# Hormonal acne: what it is, why it happens and how to tell

If your breakouts outlasted your teens and shrug off every cream, hormones are probably involved.

Hormonal acne is adult acne linked to shifts in your hormones, or to skin that reacts strongly to normal hormone levels. It usually shows up as deep, tender breakouts on the chin, jaw and lower face. It often flares at the same point in your cycle and keeps coming back despite a good routine.

At Cocoon, we group these breakouts into two patterns: one linked to high estrogen and one linked to high testosterone. [The hormonal acne quiz](/pages/quiz) suggests which one fits you in about 2 minutes.

## How do I know if my acne is hormonal?

Your acne is likely hormonal if it sits on your lower face, follows your cycle and keeps coming back despite a good routine. These five signs are the clearest. The more of them fit, the more likely hormones are involved.

- **Adult onset.** It outlasted your teens, or started in your 20s or 30s.
- **Chin, jawline and mouth.** It clusters on the lower third of your face.
- **Same point in your cycle.** It flares at a predictable time each month.
- **Deep, tender bumps.** Spots sit under the skin, hurt to touch and linger.
- **Keeps coming back.** It returns despite a good routine and a patient dermatologist.

### Where it shows up: the chin, jaw and lower face

Hormonal breakouts cluster on the chin, jaw and lower face because the oil glands there respond strongly to androgens. Androgens are the hormones that tell oil glands to make more oil. Face maps that link each zone to an organ aren't backed by evidence. Here's [why breakouts cluster on the chin and jaw](/blogs/learn/chin-and-jawline-acne). ### When it shows up: the same point in your cycle

Hormonal breakouts usually appear in the week before a period or around ovulation. To find your own pattern, note when new spots appear for 2-3 cycles. We cover [breakouts before, during and after your period](/blogs/learn/period-acne) in more detail.

### How it feels: deep, tender bumps

Hormonal breakouts feel like deep, tender bumps under the skin rather than small surface whiteheads. They often hurt before you can see them, and they're slow to settle. Squeezing rarely helps and can leave marks.

### Location and timing are the clearest tells

Location and timing are the clearest tells, and this table compares hormonal acne with the acne most people know from their teens.

| | Hormonal acne | Regular acne |
|---|---|---|
| When it starts | In adulthood, or it carries on past your teens | Usually at puberty |
| Where it sits | Chin, jawline, mouth and neck | Anywhere, often the forehead, nose and cheeks |
| Timing | Follows your cycle | No link to your cycle |
| What it feels like | Deep, tender bumps | Blackheads, whiteheads and smaller red spots |
| How it responds | Keeps coming back despite topicals | Often settles with a steady topical routine |

If several rows in the hormonal column sound like you, hormones are a likely part of the picture. Normal hormone tests don't rule it out, because skin can react strongly to normal levels.

## Could it be something else?

It could be something else: fungal acne, rosacea and perioral dermatitis can look like hormonal breakouts but need different care. This table shows how they differ.

| | What it looks like | Where it shows up | The tell |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hormonal acne | Deep, tender bumps | Chin, jaw and lower face | Follows your cycle |
| Fungal acne | Small, uniform, itchy bumps | Often the forehead, chest or back | It itches, and the bumps all look alike |
| Rosacea | Flushing and redness, with bumps but no blackheads | The centre of the face | You flush easily |
| Perioral dermatitis | Small bumps, sometimes a rash | Around the mouth and nose | It often follows steroid creams |

If your skin matches a look-alike row better than the hormonal one, a dermatologist can confirm it and suggest the right care.

Hormonal acne or fungal acne? Fungal acne itches and looks uniform, while hormonal acne is deeper, tender and tied to your cycle. Rosacea is a separate condition that causes flushing and redness, and it needs a doctor's diagnosis. [NEED: naturopath confirms the rosacea line]

Hormonal acne can also come with other signs, such as cycle changes, strong PMS and painful cramps. Mention them to your doctor, because they help build the full picture.

> **See a dermatologist first if you have:**
> - Severe, nodular or scarring acne
> - Irregular or missed periods
> - New facial or body hair
> - Thinning hair on your scalp
>
> These signs can point to conditions such as PCOS (now also called PMOS), and only a doctor can diagnose them.

## Hormonal acne often starts at a life change

Hormonal acne often starts at a life change: coming off the pill, getting a hormonal IUD, after a baby or weaning, or in perimenopause.

- **You came off the pill.** Breakouts often start 2-6 months after you stop, and some take 1-2 years to settle. You may hear this called post-pill acne.
- **You got a hormonal IUD.** Some women with Mirena or Kyleena notice breakouts on the chin, jaw or neck in the months after insertion.
- **You had a baby, or stopped breastfeeding.** Hormones shift after birth and again at weaning. Cocoon's formulas are only for after breastfeeding ends, never during pregnancy or breastfeeding.
- **You're in perimenopause.** From the late 30s to the mid 40s, deep cysts can return. Some women call it "reverse puberty".

For some women there's no obvious trigger. They had clear skin their whole life, then started breaking out at 29.

## What causes hormonal acne?

Hormonal acne starts with androgens, the hormones that tell your oil glands to make more oil. Estrogen and progesterone rise and fall across your cycle, and that shifting balance changes how your skin responds.

All acne follows the same path: extra oil, clogged pores, bacteria and inflammation. Androgens such as testosterone and DHT turn up the oil. Estrogen on its own usually lowers oil. Hormones play a part in most adult breakouts, but not every breakout follows a hormonal pattern. Our formulas are built around the two patterns below.

## What helps hormonal acne: four routes, often used together

What helps hormonal acne depends on your pattern, and four routes are worth knowing: topicals, food and lifestyle, targeted supplements and prescriptions. Topicals work on the surface, and food and lifestyle changes support your skin day to day. Spironolactone and the pill are what dermatologists recommend for hormonal acne, alongside topical retinoids and isotretinoin for severe acne. Targeted supplements are the fourth route, though the research is thin: a 2024 review in the Journal of Clinical and Aesthetic Dermatology (JCAD) found no acne trials of DIM.

If you've stopped a prescription, you still have options. Judge any supplement over about 3 cycles, or about 3 months, then check in. Stop and contact us if you get new or deeper cysts in new areas, a rash, or skin that's still worse after 6-8 weeks.

If you take the pill, have a hormonal IUD, take spironolactone or use any other medication, check with your doctor or pharmacist first.

## What else do women ask about hormonal acne?

Women also ask whether hormonal acne is genetic, what PCOS acne looks like and whether normal tests rule it out. Here are the eight questions we hear most.

### Is hormonal acne genetic? {#is-hormonal-acne-genetic}

Partly. Acne often runs in families, and your genes shape how much oil your skin makes and how strongly it responds to androgens. Genes aren't the whole story, though. Your cycle, life stage, stress and diet still move breakouts up and down, so the same skin can be calm one year and breaking out the next.

### What does PCOS acne look like?

PCOS acne looks like other hormonal acne: deep breakouts on the jaw, chin and neck. What sets PCOS (now also called PMOS) apart is the other signs, such as irregular periods, extra facial or body hair and thinning scalp hair. Only a doctor can diagnose it. We explain [how high testosterone affects your skin](/blogs/learn/high-testosterone-and-acne) in our deep-dive.

### Is forehead acne hormonal?

Sometimes, but less often than chin and jaw acne. Forehead breakouts more often come from hair products, bangs, hats and sweat. Face maps that tie each zone of the face to an organ aren't backed by evidence, so look at timing and depth, not location alone.

### At what age is hormonal acne the worst?

There's no single age. Flares tend to come at hormonal transitions: the teens, after stopping the pill, after a baby or weaning, and in perimenopause. Many women first get hormonal acne in their late 20s or 30s, even after clear skin as teenagers.

### Can hormonal acne show up right after your period?

Yes, for some women. Hormonal breakouts most often appear in the week before a period, but some women flare around ovulation or just after their period ends. Track your breakouts for 2 or 3 cycles to see your own pattern.

### Isn't estrogen good for skin?

Mostly, yes. Estrogen on its own usually lowers oil. The high-estrogen pattern is about estrogen's balance with progesterone, not estrogen being bad for you. If the pill always cleared your skin, you may lean toward the testosterone side instead.

### If my hormone tests came back normal, can my acne still be hormonal?

Yes. Many women with classic hormonal acne have normal bloodwork, because their skin reacts strongly to normal androgen levels. Results also depend on when in your cycle you're tested. [Developer: when the hormone-tests article goes live in February, add a closing sentence linking to it]
